\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Ownership beyond containment.} Mods reduce integration work by
placing cross-stage functions behind one public interface. Once installed,
all three systems support single-file maintenance in the measured tasks.
The distinction is the integration boundary, not the absence of internal
structure: a mod can span source fragments while retaining one public contract.
This gives a feature a stable unit for review and release: its semantic
definition, rewrite, and target binding evolve together while the subject graph
continues to change underneath.

\paragraph{Reuse at two boundaries.} Observations preserve results across
unrelated graph edits; specialization signatures transfer prepared bodies
across imports. As preparation shrinks, emission and native compilation occupy
a larger share of turnaround. Reusing those artifacts requires identities
covering bodies, layouts, targets, and dependencies, alongside graph invalidation.
The two boundaries answer different questions: observations decide whether a
stage runs again, while specialization identities decide whether its prepared
body remains valid. Keeping the questions separate makes a cache miss local and
gives the evaluator a precise explanation for discarded work.

\paragraph{Consistent publication.} Typed calls connect compiler roles;
transactions keep their graph edits and dependency records consistent.
Ownership determines the available capabilities, while recorded observations
determine whether previous results remain valid. This link between composition
and reuse is the design's organizing principle. Adding a role is an interface
change, adding a mod is an ownership change, and changing a graph is a
dependency change. Publishing each at one commit point lets the compiler
explain an update in the same vocabulary used to implement it: which capability
ran, which entity changed, and which result was retained.
These records also tie a measured speedup to a retained body or affected region,
and provide the state model for future artifact reuse.
They make invalidation visible to both the evaluator and the developer.
That visibility is the practical link between a modular compiler interface and
predictable update cost. The update path is therefore inspectable rather than
implicit. This makes optimization progress easier to attribute.
